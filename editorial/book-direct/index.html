Home › Roadbook › Cruise Line, Viator or Direct — What You Pay

When a ship calls at Villefranche or Cannes, there are four ways to spend the day ashore: book the excursion through the cruise line, book it through an online platform such as Viator or GetYourGuide, take a taxi at the port, or book direct with a local operator. They differ less in where they take you than in what you pay, what you travel in, and how much say you have. This is a detailed, insider’s account of all four.

Through the cruise line

The simplest option, and usually the most expensive. You book from the ship’s excursion desk or app, pay per person, and travel in a group, most often by coach. The appeal is the guarantee: the line coordinates the excursion, so if it runs late the ship waits. You pay for that assurance, and for the line’s margin on top of the operator who actually runs the tour. The trade-offs are real. A coach cannot reach a good deal of what makes the coast worth seeing — the perched villages, the narrow corniche pull-ins and the small harbours are simply off-limits to a vehicle that size — so the route is confined to where a coach can go, and the stops and timings are fixed and tightly regimented. The price per head also mounts quickly for a family or a small party.

Through an online platform

Viator, GetYourGuide and the like sit between you and the operator, and they account for the large majority of shore-excursion bookings — by most estimates around nine in ten. Their marketing budgets are such that a local operator cannot realistically outrank them, which is why a platform is the first thing almost any search returns. You gain choice and reviews, and you can compare before you sail. What you do not see as plainly is the cost of it. The platform’s commission, built into the price, is rarely below thirty per cent of each booking, and the operator keeps the rest. Most platforms also bind the operator to price parity — the same tour may not be offered for less elsewhere — so that commission does not come back to you as a lower price; it comes off what the operator has left to spend on the vehicle and the day.

That pressure shows in the vehicles. An operator working on platform margins often runs the cheapest van on the market, and there is a practical tell: a listing that seats eight is almost always the cargo-derived version of the van — usually cloth seats and the minimum of fittings and comfort — rather than the passenger model. The wide choice is a trap of its own, too. It is easy to lose an hour among listings that blur into one another, padded with stock photography and near-identical ratings, until you can no longer tell who actually provides the service or what the day will really be like. And there is no way to simply ask: a platform gives you no direct line to the operator before you commit.

A taxi at the port

There is a fourth route that reads well on paper: walk off the ship and take a taxi for the day. It worked, more or less, a long time ago; it works far less well now. To begin with, a taxi selling you a day’s touring at an agreed, off-meter price is operating outside what a taxi licence permits — the meter, not a negotiated tour fare, is the lawful basis. Then there is the language: a French taxi driver who speaks fluent English is rare, and one who can tell you anything coherent about the places you pass is rarer still. The old idea that the driver is a fount of local knowledge — the city, the restaurants, the good tables away from the tourists — is largely a myth now, whatever it was thirty years ago. A driver today spends twelve to fourteen hours at the wheel to cover the licence, the charges and a living; the part of restaurant critic is not on offer. And the price is usually inflated — often level with, or above, a professional operator, for a poorer day. A taxi is for a single transfer, not a tour.

Private or shared

One choice cuts across all of this: whether the tour is private to your party or sold by the seat and shared. Paying per head looks attractive, the figure can seem modest at first glance, and the appeal is plain enough. There are real catches.

The first is language. Buying a seat, you do not choose who fills the others or what they speak. You can find yourself in a vehicle with two English speakers, three Italian or Russian and one Spanish, and a guide obliged to switch from one to the next all day, even when fluent in all of them. It is wearing for the guide and dull for you, sitting through long stretches you cannot follow. It happens often.

The second is the wait. An operator holding, say, two bookings on an eight-seat van may keep you at the port for an hour or more while they try to fill the remaining seats from the disembarking passengers. That is time taken straight off your day ashore.

There is also the company. Sold by the seat, you never know who you will travel with — ages, fitness and habits all vary, and it is no secret that one ill-matched passenger can take the edge off the day for everyone else.

A private tour avoids all three: your party, your language, your pace, and away on time. Whether the difference in price is worth it is your call. If it gives you pause, there is a sensible middle path — you are not living in isolation aboard, so it is easy enough to find another couple or family whose company suits you and share a private vehicle between you.

Booking direct

Booking direct means dealing with the operator who runs the day — with a small firm, often the person driving. The price is for the vehicle rather than per head, so a family or a group of friends shares one fare instead of multiplying it. The itinerary is yours to shape, you can ask questions before you commit, and you keep a direct line on the day itself. It asks a little more of you at the outset: you choose the operator and confirm the details yourself. A reputable private operator builds the all-aboard time into the plan and watches the port schedule, so the cruise line’s one real advantage — the ship waiting — matters less than it first appears, provided you book someone who knows the port.

How to book direct — with us, or anyone reputable

None of this is an argument to book only with us. It is an argument to book direct, with someone who does the work properly. If you are choosing a private operator, here is what to check, whoever it is — we would rather you went to a good local than a platform, even when that local is not us.

Insist on a direct line before you pay: a person you can email, message or call, not a platform inbox. If you cannot reach anyone, move on. Ask what you will travel in, and ask for photographs of the actual vehicle — a passenger car with proper seats, not the cargo-derived eight-seat van. Confirm the tour is private to your party and that the price is for the vehicle, not per head. Check that whoever drives also guides, and in your language. Make sure they know the port and the call times for your ship, and that the all-aboard margin is built into the plan. And get the itinerary and the full price in writing before you commit.

That is how we work, and it is how any good operator works. Book whoever you can hold to it.

What it comes down to

If you want the day entirely hands off, with the ship’s guarantee above all, the cruise line earns its premium — within the limits of where a coach can go. A platform is convenient for browsing, at a markup you do not see and in a vehicle you cannot vet. A port taxi is rarely worth it for a full day. If you want the most day for your money — a private vehicle for your group, a route of your own, and a guide speaking your language — booking direct is the value option, and the one we run. We are glad to answer questions before you decide.

Questions and answers

Is it cheaper to book a shore excursion direct?

Usually, yes, for a group. A direct private tour is priced per vehicle rather than per person, so a family or small party shares one fare. The cruise line and online platforms both add a margin on top of the operator who runs the day.

Will the ship wait if I book a private tour?

The ship guarantees its own excursions. A reputable private operator manages this by building the all-aboard time into the plan and monitoring the port schedule, returning you with a clear margin. Book someone who knows the port and the call times.

What is the difference between Viator and booking direct?

Viator and similar platforms list many operators and add a commission to the price; the operator receives less, and most platforms forbid offering the same tour for less elsewhere. Booking direct means dealing with the operator yourself, with the itinerary and questions handled directly.
