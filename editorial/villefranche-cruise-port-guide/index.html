Villefranche-sur-Mer is one of the busiest cruise calls on the French Riviera, and it is a tender port. Ships anchor in the bay — one of the deepest natural harbours in the Mediterranean — and tenders bring passengers ashore to quai de l’Amiral Courbet, in the old village. This guide covers the practical points: the tender, what is at the quay, and the three ways to spend the day ashore, with an honest account of which suits which kind of day.

The tender

There is no berth at Villefranche. Ships anchor offshore and run their own tenders to quai de l’Amiral Courbet. The crossing takes ten to fifteen minutes. When several ships are in port on the same day, the tender queue lengthens in both directions — getting ashore and, more importantly, getting back. Build a margin against your all-aboard time rather than against the scheduled tender, and keep the return crossing in mind when you plan how far to travel.

At the quay

You step off the tender into the old village itself: cafes, the citadel above, the small working harbour. The railway station is a ten-minute walk uphill, above Plage des Marinières. A taxi rank is usually at the port, though numbers are limited on busy days. For orientation: Nice is six kilometres west, Monaco about twenty minutes east, and Cannes, Antibes and Saint-Paul-de-Vence around forty-five minutes west.

By train

The fastest connection to Nice. A regional TER from Villefranche-sur-Mer station reaches Nice-Ville in about seven minutes; eastward, the same line serves Monaco and Menton. Departures run roughly every thirty to thirty-five minutes, and tickets cost a few euros. Three caveats matter on a cruise day. First, trains on this line are cancelled fairly often: watch the live schedule, stay attentive, and leave a clear margin before your return to the port. Second, the line fills in summer — standing room at peak hours — and the ten-minute uphill walk to the station each way eats into a short shore window. Third, the stations do not sit in the places you have come to see: Nice-Ville is a tram ride or roughly a twenty-minute walk from the old town, and Monaco’s station has exits toward both Monte-Carlo and Le Rocher, each still a walk and Le Rocher uphill. None of it is far, but every leg needs planning — there and back.

The coastal bus, line 600

Line 600 — the route formerly numbered 100 — runs the Basse Corniche between Nice and Menton, calling at Villefranche-sur-Mer, Beaulieu-sur-Mer, Èze-sur-Mer, Cap-d’Ail, Monaco and Roquebrune. Buses come every fifteen to twenty minutes and the fare is modest. It is a scenic, inexpensive way along the coast. The caveats are real: it is slow — Nice to Menton end to end is about an hour — it is crowded in season, and it sets you down on the coast road below the perched villages. Èze village, in particular, is a steep climb above Èze-sur-Mer — not a short detour but a demanding ascent on foot of at least an hour, sometimes more, closer to a hillwalk than a stroll.

By taxi

There is a rank at the port, but Villefranche has only a handful of taxis, so when a ship tenders in, securing one is not straightforward. A taxi is sensible for a single short hop — into Nice, say. For a full day across several stops it is a different matter. It is expensive; most drivers speak little English and do not act as guides, so you have transport but no account of what you are seeing; and fares are often agreed in advance rather than run on the meter, typically well above it.

A private car, and the one constraint that decides it

The cruise day has a single hard edge: the all-aboard time. A private chauffeur-driven Mercedes V-Class is met at the tender landing with a name board, plans the route backwards from that deadline, and waits at no additional charge if the tender runs late. The price is for the vehicle — up to seven guests — rather than per person.

The honest summary: for a half-day to a single destination, the train is difficult to beat on cost and speed. For a full day across several stops against a fixed departure, a private car removes the variables the other options leave in — the queue, the connection, the walk back, the return that may not come on time.

Questions and answers

Is Villefranche-sur-Mer a tender port?

Yes. Ships anchor in the bay and tenders bring passengers ashore to quai de l’Amiral Courbet, in the old village. The tender ride takes ten to fifteen minutes.

What is the fastest way from Villefranche to Nice?

The train. A regional TER from Villefranche-sur-Mer station reaches Nice-Ville in about seven minutes. Departures run roughly every thirty to thirty-five minutes and are occasionally cancelled, so check the live schedule. The station is a ten-minute walk uphill from the quay.

How long do cruise passengers have ashore at Villefranche?

It depends on the ship. Most Villefranche calls give seven to ten hours ashore. Plan a margin before the all-aboard time, as tenders can be delayed when several ships are in port.
