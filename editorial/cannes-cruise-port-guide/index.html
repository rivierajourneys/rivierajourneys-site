Cannes cruise port What you need to know.

Cannes has always been a tender port — the harbour is too shallow for a cruise ship — and since January 2026 it is a regulated one. Ships anchor in the bay and tenders bring passengers ashore to the Vieux Port, in the centre of town. This guide covers the practical points: the new rules on larger ships, the tender, what is at the quay, and the ways to spend the day ashore, with an honest account of which suits which kind of day.

The rules from 2026

First, what did not change: the tendering. Cruise ships have never entered the harbour at Cannes — it is too shallow — and passengers have always come ashore by tender. What changed on 1 January 2026 is the arithmetic. No more than 6,000 cruise passengers may now come ashore in a single day, however many ships are in the bay, and the largest vessels — those above 3,000 passengers — are limited to one per day. The stated direction of travel is toward smaller ships still, by 2030.

For passengers, none of this changes anything. The limits are settled between the port and the cruise lines when schedules are drawn up, months in advance; if Cannes appears on your itinerary, your ship has already been counted. The day ashore looks exactly as it always has — the tender, the Vieux Port, the town at the quay. The rules decide which ships call, not what your day looks like once you are here.

The tender

Ships anchor in the Bay of Cannes and run their own tenders to the Vieux Port — the old harbour, landing at quai Laubeuf. The crossing takes ten to fifteen minutes. When several ships share the bay, the queue lengthens in both directions — getting ashore and, more importantly, getting back. Build a margin against your all-aboard time rather than against the scheduled tender, and keep the return crossing in mind when you decide how far to travel.

And one rule governs every cruise day on this coast, so it is worth stating plainly: the earlier you are ashore, the wider your day. Behind an early tender, every route is still on the table. From late morning they begin to drop away one by one: some no longer fit before the last tender back, others survive only shortened, or at a pace that turns a day out into a timed exercise. Come in early; decide on the quay if you must; but come in early.

At the quay

The tender lands you in the middle of the town itself. Le Suquet, the old quarter, rises on the hill immediately to your left; the Marché Forville, the covered market, is a few minutes’ walk behind it; the Palais des Festivals and the start of La Croisette are at the far corner of the harbour to your right. A taxi rank stands in front of the terminal, and the ferries to the Îles de Lérins — fifteen minutes to Sainte-Marguerite — leave from the same quay. The railway station is about fifteen minutes on foot. For orientation beyond town: Antibes is fifteen minutes away by car, Nice about forty, Grasse forty inland, Monaco roughly an hour along the coast.

The honest consequence: for a day spent in Cannes itself, you need no transport at all. The town is flat, compact and entirely walkable from the tender dock. The question of how to get around only arises when you want to leave it.

By train

Cannes station sits behind the town centre, about fifteen minutes on foot from the quay — a reasonable walk with empty hands, and close to impossible with luggage. Westward and eastward the line serves the whole coast: Antibes in about ten minutes, Nice in thirty to forty, Monaco in a little over an hour. Tickets cost a few euros and departures are frequent. The caveats are the same as everywhere on this line. Trains are cancelled fairly often, so watch the live schedule and leave a clear margin before your return. The line fills in summer — standing room at peak hours. And the stations do not sit in the places you have come to see: Nice-Ville is a tram ride or a twenty-minute walk from the old town, and Monaco’s station leaves you a climb from Le Rocher. For a single destination and a relaxed schedule, the train is difficult to beat. For three stops against a fixed departure, it is a chain of connections, each of which must hold.

The bus

There are coastal buses, and for completeness: they exist. The run to Nice is direct but slow, and Monaco requires a change along the way. On a day bounded by an all-aboard time, an hour or more each way on a coastal road is not a saving; it is the day. We would not plan a shore day around one.

By taxi

Taxis gather in numbers when a ship is in — supply is not the problem. The problems are of a different kind. The rank works in strict queue order: you take the next car, whatever it is, with whichever driver it comes with, and very few of them speak English. Fares for cruise passengers are generally agreed in advance rather than run on the meter, and they are not modest — a run to Monaco is commonly quoted at ninety to a hundred euros per person. Even the short hop to the railway station tends to be priced with the ship in mind. One local habit worth knowing, with no guarantee attached: walk out of the port along the quai Saint-Pierre to the rank opposite the police station, where the pricing is usually closer to ordinary. A taxi remains sensible for a single short hop; for a full day across several stops you have transport at a considerable price, and no account of what you are seeing.

A private car, and the one constraint that decides it

The cruise day has a single hard edge: the all-aboard time. A private chauffeur-driven Mercedes V-Class is met at the tender landing with a name board, plans the route backwards from that deadline, and waits at no additional charge if the tender runs late. The price is for the vehicle — up to seven guests — rather than per person. Cannes is our home port; the quay is ten minutes from where we start the day, which is worth something when tenders run early or late.

The honest summary: for a day in Cannes itself, walk — nothing else is needed. For a half-day to a single destination, the train is hard to beat on cost. For a full day across several stops — Nice, Eze and Monaco, or Grasse and Saint-Paul-de-Vence — against a fixed departure, a private car removes the variables the other options leave in: the queue, the connection, the walk back, the return that may not come on time.

Questions and answers

Is Cannes a tender port?

Yes, and it always has been — the harbour is too shallow for a cruise ship. Ships anchor in the Bay of Cannes and tenders bring passengers ashore to the Vieux Port, beside the town centre. The crossing takes ten to fifteen minutes.

Will the 2026 rules stop my ship calling at Cannes?

No. The limits — no more than 6,000 cruise passengers ashore in a day, the largest ships one per day — are settled between the port and the cruise lines when schedules are drawn up. If Cannes appears on your itinerary, your ship has already been counted, and your day ashore is unchanged: the tender, the Vieux Port, the town at the quay.

How far is the town from the Cannes tender dock?

There is no distance to speak of. Tenders land at the Vieux Port in the centre of Cannes: Le Suquet, the Marché Forville, the Palais des Festivals and La Croisette are all within a few minutes on foot.

What is the fastest way from Cannes cruise port to Nice or Monaco?

The train. Cannes station is about fifteen minutes on foot from the quay; Antibes is roughly ten minutes by rail, Nice thirty to forty, Monaco a little over an hour. For a full day across several stops against a fixed all-aboard time, a private car is the safer instrument.
